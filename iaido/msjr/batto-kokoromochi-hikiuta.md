## One’s bearing in iai

> 荒井清信公云、夫れ居合とは手近き勝負、柄口六寸の大事あり。其の形を事となし、前後左右是を知り、先ず居合抜き申す形は〔丸の中に三角〕如斯。
>
> 一、身を正しくして抜き申す形肝要也。
>
> 一、居合之位
>
> 面　薄櫻  
> 身之掛　鉢木　口傳  
> 心　水鳥

Arai Kiyonobu says, “Iai is a contest at close quarters. One essential teaching concerns *tsukaguchi rokusun*. Study that form and understand it to the front and rear, to the left and right. To begin with, the shape of the iai draw is shown here [a triangle within a circle].”

What matters is the form of the draw, with the body properly composed.

**One’s bearing in iai**

Face: pale cherry blossom\
Body: *Hachinoki* (*The Potted Trees*). Oral transmission.[^hachinoki]\
Mind: the waterbird

## The mind of the waterbird

> 右の心、水鳥に気を付け工夫あるべし。古歌に、
>
> 帆を掛けて　急ぐ小舟に　乗らずとも  
> 行く水鳥の　心知るべし
>
> 水鳥の　水に住めども　羽は濡れず  
> 海の魚とて　汐はゆくなし

To understand this state of mind, attend closely to the waterbird and reflect on its example. As the old poems say,

> With its sail set,\
> a little boat hurries on.\
> Even without boarding it,\
> the waterbird goes its way.\
> Come to understand its mind.
>
> The waterbird\
> lives on the water,\
> yet its feathers do not get wet.\
> Even a fish of the sea\
> does not taste of salt.[^waterbird]

## The mind of the white heron

> 居合太刀打共、白鷺心。口傳。古歌に、
>
> 忍ぶれど　色に出にけり　我が恋は  
> ものや思ふと　人の問ふまで
>
> 数ならぬ　心に身をば　任せねど  
> 身に従ふは　心なりけり

In iai and *tachiuchi* alike, the mind of the white heron. Oral transmission. As the old poems say,

> *Taira no Kanemori*
>
> Though I try to hide it,\
> my face has given it away,\
> this love of mine.\
> “Is something on your mind?”\
> people have even asked me.[^kanemori]
>
> *Murasaki Shikibu*
>
> I count for little.\
> My heart cannot choose\
> my lot in life.\
> Yet where my lot leads,\
> my heart follows, I find.[^murasaki]

## The essential matter of the moon in water

> 居合太刀打共、水月之大事。口傳。古歌に、
>
> 水や空　空や水とも　見え分かず  
> 通ひてすめる　秋の夜の月
>
> おしなべて　ものを思はぬ　人にさへ  
> 心をつくる　秋の初風
>
> 秋来ぬと　目にはさやかに　見えねども  
> 風の音にぞ　驚かれぬる
>
> 右の心にて悟るべし。我は知らねども敵の掛を知らする也。其處、水月白鷺、習ひ有るべし。工夫すべし。

In iai and *tachiuchi* alike, the essential matter of the moon in water. Oral transmission. As the old poems say,

> *An unnamed provincial warrior*
>
> Water or sky,\
> sky or water?\
> I cannot tell them apart.\
> It passes between them, shining clear—\
> the moon of an autumn night.[^moon]
>
> *Saigyō*
>
> Those who seldom\
> lose themselves in thought—\
> even they\
> feel their hearts stir\
> at the first wind of autumn.[^saigyo]
>
> *Fujiwara no Toshiyuki*
>
> Autumn has come—\
> my eyes can see\
> no clear sign of it,\
> yet at the sound of the wind\
> all at once I know.[^toshiyuki]

Grasp this in the spirit of the poems above. Though I do not know it myself, the enemy’s move is made known to me.[^kake] Herein lie the teachings of the moon in water and the white heron. Reflect on them.

## Winning by following the enemy

> 敵に随って勝つべき心持。古歌に、
>
> 風吹けば　柳の糸の　片寄りに  
> 靡くに付けて　過ぐる春かな
>
> 強身にて　行き当るをば　下手と知れ  
> 鞠に柳を　上手とぞ云ふ

The frame of mind for prevailing by following the enemy.[^willow-intro] As the old poems say,

> *Emperor Shirakawa*
>
> When the wind blows,\
> willow shoots as fine as thread\
> bend to one side,\
> and with each sway\
> spring slips by.[^spring-wind]
>
> Know one who meets it with force  
> to be unskilled;  
> in ball-play, the willow  
> is called the skilful one.

## The ultimate principle of iai

> 敵と出合ふ時、討勝と思ふべからず。況や恐るゝ事なし。間に毫末を加へず、電光石火の如く、ちらりと我が心に移る時、無二無三に打込む事、居合の極意也。
>
> 是、柄口六寸の習ひなり。此の間をもって敵の場へ踏み込み打つ也。
>
> 偏に大海を渡るに、陸にて行けば命を失ふ故に、船に乗り行く也。居合柄口六寸の大事、偏に彼の舟に同じ。然れ共、舟にて必ず渡海すと思ふべからず。歌に、
>
> 乗り得ても　心ゆるすな　海士小舟  
> 波間の風の　吹かぬ日ぞなき
>
> 右、波間の風、能々合点しては舟も入らず。歌に、
>
> 有と無しと　堺を渡る　海士小舟  
> 釘も草木も　抜け果てにけり
>
> 此の心にて能々工夫あるべし。口傳。

When encountering an enemy, do not think of striking and winning; still less should you be afraid. Without allowing even a hair’s breadth of interval, when, like lightning and fire struck from flint, **[it] shifts into my mind in a flash**, strike in single-mindedly, without a second or third thought. This is the ultimate principle of iai.[^utsuru]

This is the teaching of 柄口六寸 *tsukaguchi roku-sun*. By means of this **間 *ma***, step into the enemy’s ground and strike.[^ma]

It is just like crossing the great ocean: if one tried to go overland, one would lose one’s life, and therefore one boards a boat. The essential matter of 柄口六寸 in iai is just like that boat. Yet do not suppose that merely because one is aboard a boat one will necessarily cross the sea. A poem says:

> Even once aboard,  
> do not relax your mind  
> in the fisherman’s little boat;  
> there is no day when wind does not blow among the waves.

Once one fully understands the wind among the waves, even the boat is no longer needed.[^boat] A poem follows.

> The fisherman’s little boat crosses  
> the boundary between being and non-being;  
> every nail and piece of wood  
> has fallen completely away.[^kusaki]

Investigate this mind thoroughly. Oral transmission.

## Winning without drawing

> 居合抜き覚ると、早人抜かせず。故に詰合の位、能々執行有るべし。只太刀を抜けとばかり思ふべからず。抜かずして勝つの利有り。是、事〔業〕の位に教ゆべし。
>
> 人の我を捕へて抜かせじとする時、必ず抜くまじ。我を捕ゆる人を此方より取り倒すべし。或は先に捕られぬ位有るべし。敵の仕掛により、直ぐに止まり勝つ事もあり、或は其の気を避けて勝つ事も有り。皆、敵の仕掛に寄るなり。工夫有るべし。歌に、
>
> 留ると　思はばそこに　留まれよ  
> 行くと思はば　とくとくと行け
>
> 仇にのみ　人を辛しと　何か思ふ  
> 心よ我を　憂きものと知れ
>
> 右の心にて工夫有るべし。然れ共、剛刀、或は色々と理屈を云ふ人あらば、皆我が敵と知れ。然れ共、夫々に心を取られ迷ふ事なかれ。歌に、
>
> 無用なる　手詰の語を　すべからず  
> 無理な人には　勝って利はなし

Once you have learnt to draw in iai, you keep the other person from drawing.[^haya] Therefore diligently practise the positions of 詰合 *tsumeai*. Do not think only, “Draw the sword.” There is a principle of **winning without drawing**. This should be taught at the level of the techniques.[^waza]

When someone seizes me and tries to prevent me from drawing, I must not insist upon drawing. Instead, I should take down the one who has seized me. Or there is a position in which I am not seized in the first place. Depending upon the enemy’s attack, there are occasions on which one stops at once and wins, and occasions on which one avoids his *ki* and wins. All depends upon the enemy’s manner of attacking. Investigate this. A poem says:

> If you mean to stop,  
> stop in that very place.  
> If you mean to go,  
> go at once, at once.
>
> *A version of a poem by Shōgimon’in no Kohyōe no Kami*
>
> Why think only another  
> a cruel adversary?  
> O heart, know that I myself  
> am the troublesome one.[^adani]

Investigate with this mind. If someone bears a stout sword or puts forward all manner of arguments, recognise an adversary in either case.[^goto] Yet do not let your mind be taken by each of them and fall into confusion. A poem follows.

> Do not engage  
> in useless talk of *tezume*;  
> there is no benefit  
> in defeating an unreasonable man.

## 沈成躰 *Chinseitai*

> 敵、太刀打ち難き切って掛るに、沈成躰に勝ち有る事の位にて教へし。工夫有るべし。
>
> 古語に、寸の虫屈むも身を伸びんが為。歌に、
>
> 長からむ　さゝげの花は　短くて  
> 短き栗の　花の長さよ
>
> 右の心にて工夫有るべし。皆、陰合〆、陽に出る位有り。古人も心は〔　〕の内にありとのたまふなり。ものとしたる事に、勝負の位知る事なし。深く工夫有るべし。
>
> 此の沈なる躰心より、よく敵の心見ゆるもの也。
>
> 兵法に曰く、端末未見、人莫能知、と有り。歌に、
>
> 悟り得て　心の花の　開けなば  
> 尋ねん先に　色ぞ染むべき
>
> 霜閉づむ　蓬が下の　きりぎりす  
> 有るかなきかの　聲ぞ聞ゆる

When the enemy cuts in with a blow difficult to meet with the sword, teach the position in which victory lies in **沈成躰 *chinseitai***. Investigate it.[^chinseitai]

An old saying has:

> Even an inchworm bends itself  
> so that it may extend its body.

A poem says:

> The flower of the long cowpea is short,  
> while the flower of the short chestnut is long.

Investigate with this mind. In all things there is a position of gathering and closing yin, then issuing into yang. The ancients too declared that the mind lies within [this].[^kore] When a matter has become fixed as a “thing”, one cannot know the position of victory and defeat. Investigate deeply.

From this settled and lowered body-mind, the enemy’s mind becomes clearly visible.

A text on strategy says, “Until the beginning and end are apparent, no one can know.”[^kanbun] A poem follows.

> If, having attained awakening,  
> the flower of the mind should open,  
> before one even seeks it  
> its colour should already appear.
>
> *A version of a poem by Saigyō*
>
> Beneath frost-laden mugwort,  
> the cricket’s voice is heard  
> so faintly one cannot tell  
> whether it is there or not.[^cricket]

## 危道 *Kidō*

> 居合も太刀打も、敵と我と立合ふといなや、危道なくては勝事なし。然れ共、上を打たんとして下を打つ様なる事にてなし。先づ直ぐに行き、間を移し行く危道有り。此の處、詞に出し難し。数月数年、業修錬の功にて合点行くべし。能々日夜修行有るべきなり。
>
> 義経公御歌、
>
> 勢ひ来る　敵にさりなく　出合ふな  
> あひしらひして　潮を抜かせよ

In both iai and sword engagement, the instant the enemy and I face one another, there can be no victory without **危道 *kidō***. Yet this does not mean intending to strike high and then striking low. First go straight ahead; in shifting the interval as you go, there is *kidō*. This point is difficult to put into words. It must be understood through the accomplishment gained in months and years of training in the techniques. Practise it thoroughly, day and night.[^kido]

K attributes the following poem to Lord Minamoto no Yoshitsune.

> Do not meet heedlessly  
> the enemy who comes on with force;  
> receive and handle him,  
> and let his tide ebb away.

## Victory before drawing

> 居合と申すは、第一、太刀抜かぬ以前に勝つ事大事也。歌に、
>
> 抜けば切れ　抜かずは切るな　此の刀  
> たゞ切る事に　大事こそあれ
>
> あまたにて　勝れざりしと　聞きしかど  
> 心明剱の　太刀を楽しめ

In iai, the foremost matter is to win before drawing the sword. A poem says:

> If you draw, cut.  
> If you do not draw, do not cut with this sword.  
> It is in cutting itself  
> that the essential matter lies.
>
> Though I have heard that one did not prevail  
> when faced by many,  
> take delight in the sword  
> of **心明剱 *Shinmeiken***, the Sword of the Luminous Mind.[^shinmeiken]

## Drawing while travelling together (*Yukizure*)

> 行連の太刀抜く事、古人大事に云へり。工夫有るべし。歌に、
>
> 〔夏日向〕　冬日の影と　歩行べし  
> 獨り行くには　障る人なし
>
> 行違ふ　敵の足に　目を付けよ  
> 手はおのづから　留るもの也

The ancients regarded drawing the sword in **行連 *Yukizure*** as important.[^yukizure] Investigate it. A poem follows.

> Walk as in the summer sunlight  
> and the winter day’s shadow;  
> when one goes alone,  
> there is no one to obstruct the way.[^summer]
>
> Fix your eye upon the feet  
> of the enemy whom you pass;  
> the hands will stop  
> of themselves.

## Drawing when grappled

> 組合の時、太刀抜く様の事。敵組付くと早、我が身に付けて抜く事と知れ。歌に、
>
> 身に付けて　抜く習ひ有る　人はたゞ  
> 組付かぬ間に　切るとこそ聞け
>
> 居合とは　心一つに　定まらず  
> 敵の仕掛を　留る様あり

When grappling, as soon as the enemy closes upon you, know that the way to draw is to draw close against your own body. A poem says:

> Of one who possesses the teaching  
> of drawing close against the body,  
> I hear simply this:  
> cut before he can grapple.
>
> Iai is not fixed  
> in a single state of mind alone;  
> there are ways  
> of stopping the enemy’s attack.[^kokoro-katana]

## When the enemy’s sword is difficult to meet

> 敵、太刀打ち難き我に切って掛るに、早く抜き合せんとすれば、必ず負る事あり。能々工夫有るべし。歌に、
>
> 居合をば　知ったふりして　突かるゝな  
> 抜刀の道を　深く問ふべし
>
> 身の曲尺の　位を深く　習ふべし  
> 留めねど留る　事ぞ不思議や

When an enemy cuts at me in a manner difficult to meet with the sword, if I try hastily to draw and meet him, there are occasions on which I shall certainly lose. Investigate carefully. A poem says:

> Do not be thrust  
> while pretending that you know iai;  
> inquire deeply  
> into the way of 抜刀 *battō*.[^tsukaruru]
>
> Study deeply  
> the position of the body’s carpenter’s square;  
> though you do not stop it, it stops —  
> what a strange thing.[^koto]

## Kata poems

### 横雲 *Yokogumo*

> 深山には　嵐吹くらし　三吉野の  
> 花か霞か　横雲の空

> Deep in the mountains\
> a storm seems to be blowing.\
> Over Yoshino, is that blossom or haze\
> in a sky streaked with cloud?

### 虎一足 *Tora-issoku*

> 猛き虎の　千里の歩み　遠からず  
> 行くより早く　帰る足引

> The fierce tiger’s thousand-*ri* stride  
> is not far;  
> the returning foot is swifter  
> than the one going forth.

### 稲妻 *Inazuma*

> 諸共に　光りと知れど　稲妻の  
> 跡鳴る雷の　響き知られず

> Though both perceive\
> the flash of lightning,\
> the thunder rolling in its wake\
> goes unheard.

### 浮雲 *Ukigumo*

> 麓より　吹き上げられし　浮雲は  
> 四方の高根を　立ち包むなり

> Clouds blown up from the foothills  
> stand enveloping the high peaks  
> on every side.

### 山下風 *Yamaoroshi*

> 高根より　吹き下す風の　強ければ  
> 麓の木々は　雪もたまらず

> When the wind blowing down  
> from the high peaks is strong,  
> no snow settles  
> upon the trees below.

### 岩浪 *Iwanami*

> 行く舟の　舵取り直す　間もなきは  
> 岩尾の浪の　強く当れば

> The boat underway has no time  
> to straighten its helm  
> when the waves strike  
> the rocky outcrop with force.

### 鱗返 *Urokogaeshi*

> 瀧津波　瀬登る鯉の　鱗は  
> 水堰き上げて　落る事なし

> Amid the torrent’s waves,  
> the scales of the carp climbing the rapids  
> drive back the water,  
> and it does not fall.

### 浪返 *Namigaeshi*

> 明石潟　瀬戸越す波の　上にこそ  
> 岩尾も岸も　堪るものかは

> Upon the waves crossing  
> the strait at Akashi,  
> could either rocky outcrop or shore  
> endure?

### 瀧落 *Takiotoshi*

> 瀧津瀬の　崩るゝ事の　深ければ  
> 前に立添ふ　岩もなきかな

> Where the plunging torrent  
> collapses so deeply,  
> there is no rock  
> standing before it.

## Conclusion of the quoted verses

> 敵色々とありて我を騙すとも、油断する事なかれ。譬へば鞠を蹴るに同じ。我が鞠と人の鞠との色をよく見る事也。
>
> 敵をたゞ　鞠と思ひて　皆人の  
> 詰め開きせば　いかに逃がさん
>
> 本来の　事より出て　事に入り  
> あはれ知らばや　事の深さは
>
> 吹けば鳴る　吹かねば鳴らぬ　笛竹の  
> 聲の主とは　何を云ふらん
>
> 打解けて　寝るが中なる　心こそ  
> 誠の我を　顕はしにけり
>
> 引寄せて　結べば柴の　庵にて  
> 解れば本の　野原なりけり
>
> 兎に角に　云ふべき様は　なかりけり  
> 九重の塔の　上の足代
>
> 〔唱ふれば〕　仏も我も　なかりけり\
> 南無阿弥陀仏の　聲ばかりして
>
> 極楽は　遥かに遠く　聞きしかど  
> 唱へて至る　處なりけり
>
> 居合心持引歌終

The enemy has many colours and may deceive us; never be careless. It is like kicking a ball: distinguish carefully the colour of your own ball from that of the other person.

> Think of the enemy simply as a ball;  
> if you close and open the interval against every man,  
> how could any escape?
>
> Coming forth from the original matter  
> and entering into particular matters —  
> ah, if only one might know  
> the depth of the matter.
>
> Blow, and the bamboo flute sounds.\
> Without breath it is silent.\
> Whose voice, then,\
> do we hear?[^flute]
>
> The mind present  
> while one sleeps completely at ease —  
> that has revealed  
> the true self.
>
> *Traditionally attributed to Jien*
>
> Draw the brushwood together and bind it,  
> and it becomes a hut;  
> untie it,  
> and it is the original field once more.[^hut]
>
> In the end there is nothing whatever  
> that can be put into words:  
> the scaffolding  
> atop a nine-storey pagoda.
>
> *Attributed to Ippen*
>
> When one chants,  
> neither Buddha nor self remains;  
> there is only the voice  
> saying *Namu Amida Butsu*.[^tonaureba]
>
> *A version attributed variously to Senkei, Kūya and Senkan*
>
> Though I had heard  
> that the Pure Land was far away,  
> it is a place one reaches  
> through chanting.[^kikishi]

> 坪内長順　〔印〕
>
> 右歌之巻一本、貴殿御深望に依り、山川先生より相傳せしめ候。  
> 印可、其儘相讓り申候。以上。
>
> 天保二辛卯  
> 十二月吉日  
> 島村右馬丞殿

Yamakawa Yukimasa *(two seals)*

Recopied in early summer of Bunsei 10, *hinoto-i* (1827).\
Tsubouchi Chōjun *(one seal)*[^signatures]

At your earnest request, I hand over this one scroll of songs, transmitted to me by Master Yamakawa, together with the licence exactly as received. Thus.

On an auspicious day in the twelfth month of Tenpō 2, *kanoto-u* (3 January–1 February 1832).[^calendar]

To 島村右馬丞 Shimamura Umanojō.

## Textual notes

[^hachinoki]: The image for 身之掛, “bodily bearing”, comes from the Noh play 鉢木. Sano Tsuneyo, a warrior who has lost his lands, shelters a travelling monk on a snowy night. The monk urges him to spare his treasured potted plum, cherry and pine, but Tsuneyo burns them to warm his guest. Later, when Tsuneyo answers a summons to Kamakura in his worn armour, he recognises his guest as the retired regent Hōjō Tokiyori. Mitsuhira reads the allusion as meeting even an opponent with おもてなしの心, “the heart of a host”. He later suggests that 身之掛 evokes the way a Noh actor stands and the care host and guest show each other. [Text of the play][hachinoki-play] · [Mitsuhira, 2016][hachinoki-2016] · [Mitsuhira, 2019][hachinoki-2019]

[^waterbird]: K prints 海の急とて on the opening page. The reading 海の魚とて follows S. In *Seisuishō*, a related verse is the Yugyō Shōnin’s reply to Toyotomi Hidetsugu. It too has 海の魚とて, but its closing phrase differs. 汐はゆくなし can be read as the negative of the adjective しほはゆし, “salty”. [S, §2][s-2] · [*Seisuishō*, book 2][seisuisho] · [*Nihon kokugo daijiten*, 鹹映ゆい][salty]

[^kanemori]: Taira no Kanemori’s poem appears in the *Shūi wakashū* (no. 622) and the *Ogura hyakunin isshu* (no. 40). K’s opening page prints 忍れと, normalised here as 忍ぶれど. S opens 思ふれと. [*Ogura hyakunin isshu*][hyakunin] · [S, §3][s-3]

[^murasaki]: This poem opens 数ならぬ (*kazu naranu*) in both K and the *Murasaki Shikibu shū*. “I count for little” expresses the speaker’s sense of her own standing. In the headnote, Murasaki describes growing used to lamenting that her life did not go as she wished. This suggests “my lot in life” for 身 (*mi*), while the scroll’s pairing of body and mind recalls its ordinary sense of “body” or “self”. The English repeats “heart” (心, *kokoro*) and “lot” in reverse order, as the Japanese does. “I find” conveys the note of recognition in the closing けり (*keri*). The *Senzaishū* (no. 1096) reads 数ならで. S gives 数ならて心をば, omitting the first 身. [*Murasaki Shikibu shū*, poem 54 and headnote][murasaki-shu] · [*Senzaishū*, no. 1096][senzai-1096] · [Commentary on the poem][murasaki] · [S, §3][s-3]

[^moon]: Here すめる means 澄める, “shining clear”. The autumn moon shines as clearly in the water as it does overhead, and the speaker cannot tell water from sky (見え分かず). K opens 水や空 (“water or sky”) and prints すめる in kana. The National Archives copy of the *Shoku shika wakashū* (no. 184) and the Imperial Household manuscript of *Kokon chomonjū* also use kana. The edited text of *Kokon chomonjū* prints 澄める. [*Shoku shika wakashū*, no. 184, left page][shokushika-moon] · [*Kokon chomonjū*, manuscript, right page][chomonju-ms] · [Edited text and transcription, tale 185][chomonju]

    Mitsuhira’s transcription of his Sōda copy gives 水や雲 (“water or cloud”) and 住める. *Kokon chomonjū*, tale 185, credits the poem to an unnamed warrior newly arrived from the provinces. The *Shin goshūi wakashū* (no. 372) lists the poet as 読人不知, “author unknown”. Both give the theme as 水上月, the moon on water. [S, §3][s-3] · [*Kokon chomonjū*, tale 185][chomonju] · [*Shin goshūi wakashū*, no. 372][shingoshui-moon]

[^saigyo]: The *Shinkokin wakashū* credits this poem to Saigyō (西行法師). The *Seisenban Nihon kokugo daijiten* cites the poem under 心を付く to illustrate the sense “to stir someone to reflection”. [*Shinkokin wakashū*, book IV (Autumn I), no. 299][shinkokin] · [Dictionary entry, sense 2][kokoro-o-tsuku]

[^toshiyuki]: This poem by Fujiwara no Toshiyuki (藤原敏行) opens the autumn poems in the *Kokin wakashū*. The headnote reads 秋立つ日よめる, “composed on the first day of autumn”. The particle ぞ singles out the sound of the wind, and れ in 驚かれぬる marks a recognition that comes of its own accord. [*Kokin wakashū*, book IV, no. 169][kokin-autumn] · [Text and commentary][toshiyuki] · [Sanseidō’s grammatical notes][toshiyuki-grammar]

[^kake]: K reads 敵の掛を知らする也. “Move” is a broad rendering of 掛 (*kake*), which may refer here to the enemy’s initiative. Mitsuhira’s transcription of the Sōda copy reads 敵の勝をしらする也, “the enemy’s victory is made known”. The subject of 知らする is unstated. The opening 我 (“I”) suggests the recipient, hence “to me”. [S, §3][s-3] · [*Nihon kokugo daijiten*, 知らす][shirasu]

[^willow-intro]: After 心持, Mitsuhira’s transcription of the Sōda copy adds 春風を切ると云ふ, “this is called ‘cutting the spring wind’”. K goes straight to 古歌に. [S, §4][s-4]

[^spring-wind]: The *Kin’yō wakashū* credits this poem to Emperor Shirakawa (白河院). Its topic, 柳糸随風, pictures the willow’s slender branches as threads following the wind. The *yori* in 片寄り (“to one side”) also suggests 縒り, the twist of a thread. I read 靡くにつけて as the link between the sway of the branches and the sense of spring passing. K and the anthology give 過ぐる春かな, “spring slips by”. Mitsuhira’s transcription of the Sōda copy has 廻る春かな, “spring comes round”. [Anthology text, no. 28][kinyo] · [Dictionary entry for 柳の糸][yanagi-no-ito] · [Annotated text, no. 23][kinyo-willow] · [S, §4][s-4]

[^utsuru]: K reads 我が心に**移る**時, whereas S reads 我が心に**勝つ**時. The parallel 居合心立合之大事 has 敵打出す所にてちらりと気移りして勝事なり, which supports an interpretation involving the transfer or apprehension of the enemy’s *ki* or decisive cue. It does not, however, supply the omitted grammatical subject with certainty.

[^ma]: K reads 此の**間**をもって. S has 此の**習**を以て, “by means of this teaching”. S also includes an additional sentence — 然れ共只切込ていなし是に柄口六寸の習なり — which is absent from K.

[^boat]: K, p. 121, prints 舟も入らず. The English takes いらず in the sense of 要らず, “there is no need”. S transcribes 舟ものら春, read by Mitsuhira as 舟ものらず, “one does not even board the boat”. [S, §5][s-5]

[^kusaki]: K clearly prints 釘も**草木**も, literally “nails and grasses/trees”; “wood” is a cautious contextual rendering. S reads 釘も**楔**も, “nails and wedges”, which is semantically more natural. The inherited reading cannot yet be decided.

[^haya]: K, p. 121, prints 早人抜かせす. The English takes 人 as the person whom one does not let draw. The force of 早 in this construction is uncertain. S transcribes はやく抜せす. [S, §6][s-6]

[^waza]: K is unclear at 是事さの位. S gives 是事（業）の位. The translation follows 業 *waza* provisionally.

[^goto]: K, p. 121, prints 剛刀, literally “a stout sword”. The English takes this to describe its bearer. S reads 剛力, “brute strength”. [S, §6][s-6]

[^adani]: The *Gyokuyō wakashū* credits the love poem beginning よそにのみ to Shōgimon’in no Kohyōe no Kami (章義門院小兵衛督). The other four phrases recur here. An early printed text, 佩戒女小学, has the opening あだにのみ. A modern selection credits that version to Ikkyū. [*Gyokuyō wakashū*, text and attribution][gyokuyo] · [Transcription of 佩戒女小学][adani-early] · [Modern attribution to Ikkyū][adani-ikkyu]

[^chinseitai]: S includes an explanatory annotation absent from K: 沈成躰 means lowering one’s body against the enemy and settling one’s *ki*.

[^kore]: K, p. 121, leaves a bracketed space after 心は. The Japanese preserves that space. The English supplies “[this]” from 之 in S’s 心は之内二有り. [S, §7][s-7]

[^kanbun]: The eight characters 端末未見，人莫能知 on p. 121 of K reproduce a sentence in the 上略, the first part of the *Three Strategies* (三略, *Sanryaku*). 端末 takes in both the beginning and the end. [*Sanryaku*, 上略, §2][sanryaku] · [S, §7][s-7]

[^cricket]: Saigyō’s *Sankashū* places the cricket beneath 葎 (*mugura*, tangled weeds). K has 蓬 (*yomogi*, mugwort) and changes the closing phrase from 聲きこゆなり to 聲ぞ聞ゆる. [Saigyō, *Sankashū*][sankashu]

[^kido]: K, p. 121, reads 危道, 業 and 義経公. At the first occurrence of 危道, S gives a blank (□) with 是 as Sōda’s suggestion. S also has 事 and 義公. In 2020 Mitsuhira explicitly attributed the three readings found in K to Kimura Eijū’s text. A title page or imprint for K would help establish whether it comes from Kimura’s edition. 詭道, “the deceptive way”, is Mitsuhira’s conjecture. [S, §8][s-8] · [Mitsuhira’s 2016 interpretation][kido-2016] · [His 2020 comparison with Kimura][kimura] · [Kimura’s volume in CiNii Books][kimura-catalog]

[^shinmeiken]: The exact construction of the preceding line is uncertain. 心明剱 is retained as the named principle *Shinmeiken* and glossed literally.

[^yukizure]: K, p. 122, names 行連 (*Yukizure*, “travelling together”). S gives 行違 (*Yukichigai*, “passing one another”). Both are separately named in the school’s list. K itself uses the verb 行違ふ in the second poem. [S, §8][s-8] · [School list](README.md#battō-kokoromochi-no-koto)

[^summer]: K clearly prints 夏日なく, which appears corrupt in context. S reads 夏日向, followed by 冬日の影. The normalised Japanese and translation provisionally follow S; the brackets mark the emendation.

[^kokoro-katana]: K reads 心一つ, “one mind” or “a single state of mind”. S and parallel song traditions read 刀一つ, “the sword alone”. The readings yield materially different teachings, so neither has been silently substituted for the other.

[^tsukaruru]: K has 突かるゝな, “do not be thrust”, and 抜刀の道. S has つかるゝな and 居合の道. K is followed here.

[^koto]: The abbreviated 事 before ぞふしぎや on p. 122 of K is also used in 落る事なし and 崩るゝ事の farther down the same page. S writes 事ぞふしぎや. [S, §8][s-8]

[^flute]: K, p. 123, prints こゑの主しとは, normalised here as 聲の主とは. 主 (*nushi*) is the owner or source of the voice. S has 声の良とハ, and Mitsuhira’s reading underneath gives 声の良しとは, which asks about the quality of the sound. His 2020 discussion gives 聲の主とハ. [S, §13][s-13] · [January 2020 discussion][flute-2020]

[^hut]: The *Ikkyū oshō hōgo* credits a version of the hut poem to Jien under his posthumous name, Jichin (慈鎮). A study of the *Harusameshō* questions the same credit in that collection and lists the source of its verse as unidentified. [Akita Prefectural Library on the *Ikkyū oshō hōgo*][hut-jien] · [Study of the *Harusameshō*, pp. 12–13][hut-study]

[^tonaureba]: K, p. 123, prints とのおれは. S gives 唱ふれハ. The *Ippen shōnin goroku* recounts that Ippen presented this poem, beginning となふれば, to the Zen teacher Hottō Kokushi. The next line gives the words he chants. [S, §13][s-13] · [Passage from the *Ippen shōnin goroku*][ippen-goroku] · [Tōkōji’s account][ippen]

[^kikishi]: K, p. 123, reads 聞しかと, normalised as 聞きしかど, “though I had heard”. S transcribes ゆきしかど, “though I went”. Mitsuhira gives 聞きしかど in the reading beneath that transcription. In an older version, つとめて (“with diligence”) stands where K has 唱へて (“through chanting”). The *Shūi wakashū* (no. 1343) credits that poem to Senkei (仙慶), the *Senzaishū* to Kūya (空也), and the *Fukurozōshi* to Senkan (千観). [S, §13][s-13] · [The three attributions in the *Jōdoshū zensho*][pureland-credits] · [Fusenji on the older wording][pureland-verse]

[^signatures]: On p. 123, Yamakawa’s name and two seals stand to the right of the Bunsei date. Tsubouchi’s name and one seal follow the copying formula 初夏改寫者也. The layout favours Tsubouchi as the 1827 copyist. The transfer statement names Yamakawa as the source of transmission. The personal-name readings follow the companion translation. A study of Shimamura’s diary identifies Tsubouchi as his teacher and Yamakawa as Tsubouchi’s teacher. [*Iai kongen no maki*](iai-kongen-no-maki.md) · [Study of Shimamura’s diary][diary]

[^calendar]: The twelfth lunar month of Tenpō 2 began on 3 January 1832 and ended on 1 February 1832 in the Gregorian calendar. 吉日 gives only “an auspicious day”. [National Astronomical Observatory of Japan, first day][calendar-first] · [Last day][calendar-last]
